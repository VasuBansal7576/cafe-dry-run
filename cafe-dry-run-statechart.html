What we are building, and for whom

One connected flow: spot, demand range, simulated shop, runway. Staff and equipment are the two biggest variable costs, and a first-time founder has to decide them before the lease is signed.

The problem

A first-time cafe founder makes linked decisions with incomplete information: is the spot busy enough, can one barista serve the rush, do we need another machine, how long does cash last if demand is lower than hoped?

Existing tools cover pieces (financial scenarios in one, restaurant simulation in another). Our angle is connecting them in an inspectable experience before the cafe exists. We do not claim to have invented restaurant simulation.

Who it is for

  • Primary: a first-time cafe founder with a shortlisted spot, a cash figure and a rough rent, but no operations experience.
  • Co-operator: the agent, which runs scenarios and explains them.
  • Not for: operators who already have point-of-sale data, or anyone wanting a sales prediction.

The question it answers

"What would have to happen for this cafe to work?"

Not "will this cafe succeed?". The first can be shown truthfully with limited data. The second needs evidence we do not have.

It promises

  • A repeatable simulated Saturday you can inspect and edit.
  • The bottleneck named, with the reason it moved.
  • Break-even and runway that follow from the staff and equipment you chose.
  • Every input labeled: observed, public, user estimate or illustrative.

It never claims

  • That the map predicts sales or footfall.
  • That one simulated Saturday equals a month (no multiplying by 30).
  • That faster service always earns more: only if it saves customers who would have left, and that assumption is visible.
  • Numbers the agent invented: every figure comes from an action.

Architecture

The language model orchestrates and explains; it never computes a number. The simulator and cash model are pure TypeScript called by actions on the server, with no ML model. Actions return or store an event timeline for the UI to play back.

Data, and how much to trust it

No model is trained and no GPU is needed. Public data gives context. Money, demand and timings come from the founder or are labeled defaults.

ObservedPublic dataUser estimateIllustrative default

Weakest-link rule. A derived number inherits the least trustworthy tag among its inputs. If a result depends on an illustrative service time, the result is shown as illustrative until that input is replaced by an observation.
Public data

Map and nearby places

OpenStreetMap and Overture Places, preloaded as a static snapshot so the demo never waits on a public API.

Context on the map only. Never converted into customers. Businesses can be missing or outdated. Attribution and licence terms apply.

Public data

Competitor and price context

Kaggle Zomato Bangalore restaurants (about 51.7k rows in the large file, older scrape). Used to show a nearby price level and presence of competitors.

Vote counts are cumulative, not footfall. Check each dataset's licence before bundling a subset, and list it in the submission.

User estimate

Hourly customer arrivals

Founder enters low, base and high customers per hour plus opening hours. The Saturday curve is an editable shape.

Defaults are labeled illustrative until edited.

Observed

Pedestrian count (optional)

If the founder has a count, arrivals = passers-by x assumed conversion rate. If not, the app runs in reverse and shows the conversion break-even would require.

Public counts exist for some places, but not for arbitrary Indian storefronts, so this input is optional.

Illustrative default

Service times and patience

Seconds per task (order, grind, pull shot, steam milk, hand-off) and how long customers wait before leaving.

Replace with the founder's own timings or a stopwatch session. These drive the result most, so they stay visible.

Public data

Equipment capacities

Group heads, grinders, steam wands. Example: one commercial grinder's maker quotes 4 to 7 g/s, so an 18 g dose is roughly 3 to 5 seconds of grinding before handling.

Defaults use published specifications where available and are not tuned to favor a purchase. The demo shows whatever the model recommends.

User estimate

Money inputs

Rent, wages, menu prices, ingredient costs, startup costs, available cash. Weekday and weekend assumptions are separate; startup and recurring costs are separate.

Not used

Deliberately excluded

  • Google Popular Times: relative busyness, not a customer count.
  • WorldPop: modeled residents, not passers-by or buyers.
  • Foursquare OS Places: now gated on Hugging Face, so not a dependency.
  • Live Overpass calls during the demo: rate-limited, so use the snapshot.

Simulation rules

Small enough to verify, rich enough that the bottleneck can surprise us.

  1. Arrivals follow a time-varying random process driven by the hourly range, using a seeded random generator.
  2. Tasks per drink: order, grind, pull shot, steam milk, hand-off. Each needs a person. Grind, pull and steam also need a machine.
  3. People are shared resources. One barista may take orders, grind and steam. A group head is busy during extraction while the person is free for other work.
  4. Customers leave if the wait exceeds their patience. This counts as lost orders, and the patience assumption is visible.
  5. Bottleneck is where tasks wait longest before starting, not simply the busiest machine. Shared barista time can be the constraint even when every machine looks underused.
  6. Fair comparison: alternatives use the same seed and the same arrivals. Report wait p50 and p90 over many runs (target 100).
  7. Revenue effect: faster service adds revenue only if the saved customers would otherwise have left. That toggle is on screen.
  8. Validation: test a single-station case against Erlang-C within a stated tolerance.
  9. Runway: never multiply one Saturday by 30. Weekdays, weekends, startup and recurring costs each have their own assumptions.

Demo script for the public Clips recording

  1. Open the app in single-user demo mode or with a seeded demo account. Pick a Bengaluru spot. Nearby places appear with a visible source and gaps stated.
  2. Enter rent, cash, opening hours and low, base, high customers per hour. Tags appear on every number.
  3. Play the simulated Saturday. Queue grows, customers are lost, the bottleneck is named.
  4. Ask the agent: "Should I add a barista or buy another grinder?" Watch it create two variants by editing the same controls you can edit.
  5. Challenge it: "Only schedule the second barista from 10 to 12." Edit the shift by hand. The agent re-runs and shows deltas in wait, staff cost and cash.
  6. Show the assumption that could reverse the recommendation, then the break-even and monthly cash view. Show the recommendation the model actually produces.

Build order

  1. Scaffold with npx @agent-native/core@latest create; decide between single-user demo mode and a seeded demo account.
  2. Resource simulator and cash model with tests (same seed, same result).
  3. One cafe, one Saturday, the assumptions panel and the animated queue.
  4. Actions, then the agent driving them, then the comparison view.
  5. Map snapshot layer, as context only. It must never block the demo.
  6. Polish, record Clips, submit and confirm receipt.

How judges score it, and where we answer

Usefulness and execution, 40%

A complete working flow from spot to runway. Group A of the checklist.

Creativity and design, 20%

An animated queue where you see the bottleneck move and understand why.

Innovation, 20%

The agent builds counterfactuals and names the assumption that would flip its advice.

Cleverness and use of Agent-Native, 20%

One action set shared by UI, agent and MCP over one scenario store. Group B.
